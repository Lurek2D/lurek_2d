# Shopkeeper Management

**Category:** Commerce and economy  
**Reference games:** Recettear, Moonlighter, Potionomics, Winkeltje: The Little Shop  
**Document type:** Technical game design and architecture

## Design target

A 2D shop management game where the player buys or crafts goods, prices inventory, lays out shelves, serves customers, manages reputation, and reinvests profits. The product is smaller than a full city builder but deeper than an idle clicker: every day should create pricing, stocking, customer-flow, and upgrade decisions.

## Market positioning

Use the reference set to define expectations around readable economics, cozy but consequential loops, and visible customer behavior. Target itch.io with a small shop, one product category, and a satisfying day-end report. Target Steam with multiple product lines, shop upgrades, events, customer archetypes, debt or rent pressure, balance telemetry, and enough mid-term goals to support multi-session progression.

## Runtime architecture

Split the game into planning, open-shop, closing-report, and upgrade scenes. Planning lets the player place fixtures, set prices, queue crafting or purchasing, and assign stock. Open-shop runs a timed simulation: customers spawn from demand rules, navigate through the shop, inspect shelves, decide whether to buy, queue for checkout, and produce satisfaction or churn records. Closing-report aggregates revenue, missed demand, stockouts, reputation changes, rent, events, and unlock progress.

The economy should be explicit and inspectable. Product definitions own category, base cost, quality, shelf life, demand tags, display size, and margin expectations. Customers own budget, desire tags, patience, price tolerance, and path target. Shelves own capacity, accepted categories, visible stock, and reservation counts. Pricing should produce explainable outcomes through modifiers, not random black boxes.

Use `lurek.tilefield` for blocked cells, fixture footprint, queue slots, and customer path costs. Use `lurek.ui` for shelf inspector, price editor, inventory ledger, day report, and upgrade catalog. Keep the render layer passive: it shows customer state, shelf stock, checkout queues, and alerts generated by the simulation.

## Data and content model

- Author products, suppliers, customer archetypes, events, shop upgrades, fixtures, and demand curves as data.
- Store durable shop layout, fixture IDs, inventory lots, prices, reputation, debts, calendar day, and unlocked suppliers in `lurek.save`.
- Keep daily telemetry as summarized records: visits, conversion rate, stockouts, average margin, queue abandonment, and satisfaction.
- Do not save transient customer path nodes, animation state, UI widgets, or derived demand caches.

## Technical design notes

- Use `lurek.pathfind` for customer routing and queue movement; cache paths by fixture target when the layout is unchanged.
- Use `lurek.ai` utility scoring for purchase intent, shelf choice, checkout priority, and worker task assignment.
- Use `lurek.dataframe` for product and economy tables because balance iteration will be frequent.
- Use `lurek.charts` or structured `lurek.ui` reports to make margin, demand, and reputation readable to players.

## Vertical slice acceptance

The first slice should include one shop room, ten products, three customer archetypes, shelves, checkout, price editing, customer pathing, stockouts, day timer, revenue/profit report, one upgrade, one event day, and save/load.

## Risks

The major risk is invisible economy behavior. If players cannot explain why customers buy, leave, or complain, the design will feel arbitrary. Keep every economic result attributable to product fit, price, stock, queue time, reputation, or event modifiers.
